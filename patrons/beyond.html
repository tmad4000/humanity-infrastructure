Beyond software

The whole commons, funded the way we choose.

Patronage starts with software because software is easy to count and easy to share. The idea underneath is older and wider: the things we all rely on should be paid for by the people who rely on them, in proportion to how much they matter, with the accounts open.

What counts as the commons

Anything that gets more valuable when more people can use it, and that nobody can fence off without making it worse.

  • Knowledge. Open textbooks, reference works, guides to health and repair, research anyone can read.
  • Tools. Free software, open hardware designs, open standards and the data they run on.
  • Places and services. Libraries, parks, community gardens, repair cafés, the local composting scheme.
  • Coordination. The quiet work of maintaining, moderating, translating and teaching that holds the rest up.

Most of this is funded by accident: a grant here, a volunteer there, a company's goodwill until the goodwill runs out.

The same model, applied wider

Take the three parts of the patron subscription and remove the word "software".

  • One flat contribution. You give a fixed amount for the commons you use, however much you use it.
  • A default that follows use. Money goes where people actually go, measured in aggregate and never per person.
  • Your own override. If something matters more to you than the numbers show, you move your share toward it.

The override is what makes this different from a tax or a charity drive. It is a standing vote that costs nothing extra to cast.

A discretionary share of public money

Now imagine a small part of what you already pay in tax working the same way. Most of it funds what it funds today. A slice is yours to direct among public goods. If the subway keeps failing you, you move some of your slice away from it, and toward the neighbourhood composting project that works. The agency notices. So does the composting project.

This is not a thought experiment. Several countries already let taxpayers direct a fixed share of income tax, and many cities let residents decide part of the budget.

Precedents

  • Italy. Three boxes on the tax return. Otto per mille sends 0.8% of your income tax to the state or to a recognised religious body. Cinque per mille sends 0.5% to a nonprofit or research body you name, or to your own municipality. Due per mille sends 0.2% to a registered political party.
  • Poland. Taxpayers can direct 1.5% of their personal income tax to a public benefit organisation of their choice. It was 1% until the returns filed in 2023.
  • Hungary. 1% of income tax to a civil organisation, and a further 1% to a church or a designated state fund.
  • Spain. Two tick boxes on the return: 0.7% to the Catholic Church, 0.7% to social causes. Ticking both sends 0.7% to each.
  • Japan. Furusato nozei, the "hometown tax": a donation to a municipality you choose is deducted from your own income and resident taxes, up to a cap, so part of your tax follows your choice of place.
  • Participatory budgeting. Residents decide directly how part of a city budget is spent. It began in Porto Alegre, Brazil, in 1989. New York City has run it in council districts since a 2011 pilot. Paris pledged about 5% of its investment budget to it for 2014 to 2020.
  • Public money for open source. Germany's Sovereign Tech Fund, now the Sovereign Tech Agency, pays for the maintenance of open-source infrastructure with federal funds.
  • Matching by head count. Quadratic funding, used by Gitcoin Grants, shares out a matching pool according to how many people back a project, not only how much they give.

What these schemes teach

  • Small percentages are enough to matter. Half a percent, chosen by millions of people, is a serious budget and a clear signal.
  • The default decides most of the outcome. Many people never tick a box. What happens to an unassigned share is the most important rule in the design.
  • Eligibility is where trust is won or lost. Every scheme above keeps a public register of who can receive money, and argues about it.
  • Publish everything. Totals per recipient, every year. The schemes that last are the ones anyone can audit.

Why start with software

Software has clean edges: a project either has an open license or it does not, use can be counted without watching anyone, and a payment of one dollar can be split a hundred ways at no cost. It is the cheapest place to find out whether a usage default with a personal override produces a split people consider fair. If it does, the method is ready for larger things. If it does not, we will have learned that with tens of dollars instead of tens of millions.

What this page is not

It is not a tax scheme, and nothing here is tax-deductible. Patrons of the Commons is a voluntary subscription that is not yet taking payments. The public-money part is a direction, written down so it can be argued with.
